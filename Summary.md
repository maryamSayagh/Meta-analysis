A researcher's most traumatising experience is a project that had so much potential, consumed more time than one can afford, and then goes blank, yielding mediocre to no insights at all. In independent research, repeated versions of this experience can quietly erode confidence. When your mindset goes too deep in the black hole of debilitating self-doubt, you begin to ask yourself: Am I a bad researcher? Am I even a researcher at all?

One way to keep going is to shift the inner dialogue: Fine. Let’s work today as a “bad” researcher. Most of us are, anyway. That reframing can temporarily reduce the anxiety and get you moving again. But if we step back, we might notice a pattern forming from this phenomenon and it’s a once-in-a-lifetime experience for every researcher.

I started revisiting my old failed work, extracting the lesson quickly, then stress-testing it on my current projects — an out-of-sample check, basically. What I learnt liberated me from what I thought was no more than a psychological state; uncertainty and perfectionism stopped deciding how I do my research. Realised I was trying to solve a problem, accusing the wrong suspect. In this article, I’ll argue that what we label as personal psychological issues slowing researchers are no more than a systemic reflection of research methodologies on cognitive states.

FoMO, Fear of Missing Out, is typically defined in the social-psychology literature as anxiety driven by the perception that others may be benefiting from experiences or information you’re not accessing. The underlying mechanism is that when the environment continuously produces valuable updates, individually opting out is hard. In research, this maps to persistent concern about missing new publications, methods, or datasets that could undermine — or redirect — work already in progress.

The traditional Waterfall methodologies align with a FoMO-driven psychology: a belief that no step can begin until the previous one is fully understood and completed. In fact, some of these methodologies are a perfect harvesting environment for the FoMO phenomenon. Socially, a community accessing infinite time softens that pressure — there’s always a chance to catch up. Traditional research methodologies operate on the opposite condition: a finite timeline inside an expanding field. Researchers exposed to these methodologies are forced into this ambiguous condition and are often blamed for falling behind.

That’s the “choose your poison” dilemma. Either you keep chasing updates to preserve scientific coverage (and the work becomes too slow to ship), or you optimise for speed and visibility (and the work risks becoming shallow or opportunistic).

There’s a third path: switch to a methodology that assumes you can’t be complete, and builds progress around iteration instead of certainty. When you do that, FoMO stops being something the workflow harvests — and becomes something the workflow contains.

The link between FoMO and the waterfall approach isn’t limited to slowing research. This linear mindset offers an illusion of control that briefly soothes FoMO anxiety, but it also traps researchers in a cycle of over-preparation and delayed execution. When you believe — a priori — that you must fully complete each step before starting the next, you also increase the risk of confirmation bias; you begin shaping each step to protect the probability of validating your initial hypothesis.

What starts as “thoroughness” can then morph into sunk-cost commitment. After three months of heavy investment, admitting that the project won’t produce the expected outcome becomes psychologically expensive. Even if you realise the work is drifting in a different direction — not necessarily the opposite, just not what you intended — you’re less likely to accept the result and consider stopping. Instead, you push harder, trying to force the project back into the original plan.

Moreover, what I describe here as ‘thorough research’ have a large set of definitions. Does it mean a literature review should extract the insights every paper ever had? What if I miss the one paper that proves me wrong? In this case, one way to increase the chance of not getting anything wrong is a systematic review, then the researcher is exposed to another risk of wasting resources to end up with a conclusion that the only two papers that were already extracted manually are more than just noise. I experienced this harshly while doing feature engineering and benchmarking in choosing which is more representative (testing as many as possible). Completeness as a precondition for progress, in a field where completeness is never achievable, is a perfect harvesting environment for FoMO. The Waterfall model appeals to the desire for total clarity before action, is cognitively giving an illusion of completeness.

Interestingly, some of the fastest-moving research communities didn’t wait for academia to redesign the process. . The scientific field moved from research analysis to research engineering, the methodology we thought is only necessary to sync development to operations, developers also brought the agile mindset — not limited to the industry’s methods, it translated to imported software habits: short feedback loops, baseline-first thinking, open code, rapid replication, continuous evaluation. We often credit AI alone for the acceleration of the last 5–8 years, but a large part of the speed-up came from workflow: systems that stopped harvesting FoMO and started working around it. The methodology we thought is only necessary to optimise production is used to sell mathematical models, it shaped the way we create AI models and solutions. And if it emerges to other fields outside AI and computer science, they can quickly reach a peak too.

That exemplifies the availability of alternatives, and experienced researchers are already adopting it regardless of noticing the similarity with agile principles or not. For a wandering independent researcher like me, such a conclusion can guide us, especially if mentoring is not available. Short iterations create frequent feedback and “mini-closures,” so uncertainty doesn’t accumulate into anxiety but rather gets processed in small, testable chunks. Over time, this trains a key psychological shift when researchers redefine “complete” as “sufficient for the next iteration.” Mindfulness and self-regulation studies show that self-controlled decision-making mitigates FoMO by anchoring attention to present goals instead of hypothetical information gaps.

Methodological evolution mirrors cognitive evolution. The real focus of this article is not to showcase how a methodology is stronger than another; sometimes, both are not appropriate, therefore the decision is not binary — adopt or not adopt. Viewing research as a free curiosity loop where perfection is not imposed, and uncertainty is embraced, is a psychological state. Although I argued earlier that FoMO is not merely psychological, the reflection of research methodologies on cognitive states is.
